# Color Intelligence & Visual Judgment

## Objective

Use color with restraint and purpose. A technically valid palette is not necessarily a tasteful or coherent one.

## Establish a system first

Before styling components:
- Inspect the product's existing brand and design system.
- Identify the primary brand accent, neutral surfaces, text colors and semantic colors.
- Define a coherent set of tones for light and/or dark themes where required.
- Keep hue, saturation and lightness relationships intentional.
- Reuse design tokens instead of inventing component-specific colors.

Do not replace an established product identity without a clear reason or user approval.

## Palette discipline

Avoid:
- Arbitrary combinations of unrelated accent colors.
- Childish, crayon-like or cartoonish palettes unless explicitly requested.
- Muddy, dirty-looking or unintentionally washed-out colors.
- Excessively saturated colors that compete for attention.
- Generic AI palettes applied without considering the product.
- Introducing a new color merely to make a component look different.

Prefer a controlled palette with a clear identity, useful neutral range and carefully chosen accents. A product may need more than one accent or a broad data palette; use additional colors only when the information or product genuinely requires them.

## Semantic colors

Semantic colors must be selected to fit the overall visual system.

- Destructive: use a deliberate destructive tone, not an arbitrary harsh red.
- Success: use a balanced success tone.
- Warning: use a controlled warning tone.
- Information: use a coherent informational tone.

A destructive action does not always need a permanently bright-red filled button. Depending on context, use a neutral action with a restrained destructive label or icon, and make destructive emphasis clearer on hover, confirmation or in the relevant state.

Do not hide the meaning of destructive actions. Keep labels, confirmation and feedback clear.

## Surfaces and differentiation

- Use background and surface tones to establish hierarchy and depth.
- Use contrast to distinguish panels, selected items and interactive regions.
- Do not rely on borders for every separation.
- Avoid too many neighboring surface tones that make the interface look patchy.
- Use elevation only where it clarifies layering.
- Do not add gradients, shadows or glow effects as decoration.

## Contrast and accessibility

- Maintain readable text and control contrast.
- Do not sacrifice accessibility for subtlety.
- Check text, icons, focus indicators, disabled states and interactive boundaries.
- Do not communicate status through color alone; use labels, icons or other cues where needed.

## Review

Before completion, inspect the actual rendered palette:
1. Do the colors work together as one system?
2. Is saturation controlled and consistent?
3. Is the accent used selectively?
4. Do semantic colors fit the product identity?
5. Do surfaces create clear hierarchy without looking patchy?
6. Is contrast accessible in all important states?
7. Does the result look mature and commercially credible rather than randomly colorful?

Correct arbitrary or clashing choices before delivery.
