# Professional UI Composition

## Objective

Do not merely assemble buttons, inputs, cards and containers. Compose a coherent interface around the user's workflow, content and information hierarchy.

## Proportions and control sizing

- Size controls according to their purpose, content, platform and interaction needs.
- Do not stretch buttons or inputs to fill available width without a functional or deliberate layout reason.
- Use content-sized buttons where appropriate; use full-width actions only when the context benefits from them.
- Inputs should have widths appropriate to their expected content and surrounding form layout.
- Avoid unnecessarily tall controls, oversized icons and excessive internal padding.
- Keep related controls consistent in height, alignment and treatment.
- Preserve adequate touch targets and accessibility even in dense interfaces.

## Alignment and spatial relationships

- Establish clear alignment anchors for the screen.
- Align related labels, controls, columns and actions consistently.
- Group related items through proximity.
- Separate unrelated groups through deliberate spacing, surface changes or structure.
- Use a consistent spacing scale rather than arbitrary gaps.
- Avoid awkward floating elements, accidental offsets and uneven edges.
- Use available screen space intentionally; neither inflate whitespace nor cram content without reason.
- Prefer layout structures that reflect the user's workflow and content relationships.

## Visual hierarchy

Use a deliberate combination of:
- Position and grouping.
- Typography, weight and size.
- Spacing and density.
- Surface and tonal contrast.
- Component size and emphasis.
- Color, used selectively.

Not every element should have equal visual weight. Make the primary task clear without making everything oversized or brightly colored.

## Borders, outlines and surfaces

- Do not outline every input, card, panel or section by default.
- Use spacing, background contrast, tonal surfaces or typography when they communicate separation more cleanly.
- Apply borders only when they clarify boundaries, support interaction or improve accessibility.
- Avoid turning every group into a card.
- Do not add shadows, gradients or decorative effects without a clear purpose.
- Use elevation and layering consistently when the product benefits from them.

## Component consistency

Maintain consistent and reusable rules for:
- Heights and widths.
- Padding and spacing.
- Typography.
- Radius.
- Icon size and alignment.
- Surface colors.
- Hover, focus, active, selected, disabled and error states.

Do not invent a new visual treatment for each section. Respect an existing, intentional design system.

## Responsive composition

- Recompose layouts for the actual screen size; do not simply shrink desktop layouts.
- Preserve task priority and logical grouping on mobile.
- Avoid horizontal overflow and controls that become awkwardly stretched.
- For dense professional tools, use purposeful panels, drawers, tabs or progressive disclosure rather than squeezing every control onto one screen.

## Focus and accessibility

- Do not blindly accept browser-default styling as the finished design.
- Design focus, hover, active, selected and disabled states to fit the product.
- Never remove visible keyboard focus without providing an accessible replacement.
- Maintain readable contrast, clear labels and usable interaction targets.

## Review before completion

Inspect the rendered interface, not just the source code. Check alignment, proportions, spacing, density, responsive behavior and interaction states.

Ask:
- Do the proportions feel intentional?
- Are elements aligned and grouped correctly?
- Are controls larger than necessary or too small to use?
- Is hierarchy clear without excessive decoration?
- Are borders and cards genuinely useful?
- Does the interface feel coherent and production-ready?

Fix visible composition problems before declaring the work complete.
